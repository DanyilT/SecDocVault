Glossary
The following terms recur throughout this thesis and are defined here for clarity:
-	AES-256-GCM — the Advanced Encryption Standard operating with a 256-bit key in Galois/Counter Mode, an authenticated-encryption scheme providing both confidentiality and integrity.
-	PBKDF2 — Password-Based Key Derivation Function 2, a function that derives a cryptographic key from a password by applying a pseudorandom function iteratively with a salt, raising the cost of brute-force attacks.
-	TEE / Android Keystore — a Trusted Execution Environment is an isolated processing region providing hardware-backed security; the Android Keystore uses it, where available, to store keys such that they cannot be exported from the secure boundary.
-	Zero-knowledge architecture — in the applied sense used here, a design in which the service provider stores only encrypted data and never possesses the means to read the plaintext it holds.
-	Anonymity, pseudonymity, unlinkability, unobservability — the graded privacy properties defined by (Pfitzmann and Hansen, 2010): respectively, being unidentifiable within a set of subjects; acting under a persistent identifier not tied to a real identity; the inability of an observer to relate two items of interest; and the inability of an observer to detect whether an item of interest exists at all.
-	Device fingerprinting — the identification or re-identification of a device or user from a combination of observable characteristics rather than from an explicit identifier.
-	Data remanence — the residual persistence of data after attempts have been made to delete or erase it.
-	PII / PHI — Personally Identifiable Information and, in the healthcare context, Protected Health Information.
-	STRIDE — a threat-modelling taxonomy categorising threats as Spoofing, Tampering, Repudiation, Information disclosure, Denial of service and Elevation of privilege.
-	CVSS — the Common Vulnerability Scoring System, a standardised framework for rating the severity of security vulnerabilities.
-	Attack surface — the totality of points at which an unauthorised actor could attempt to enter, extract data from, or otherwise interact with a system.
-	EXIF — Exchangeable Image File Format; metadata embedded in image (and audio) files by cameras and smartphones, which can record device model, timestamp and, where location services are enabled, precise GPS coordinates.
-	Firebase — Google's mobile and web application development platform; SecDocVault uses its Authentication and Cloud Storage services for user sign-in and for storing encrypted documents.
-	Quasi-identifiers — attributes that are not unique identifiers in isolation but that, in combination (for example postcode, gender and date of birth), can single out an individual.
-	k-anonymity — a data-release model (Sweeney, 2002) under which each record is indistinguishable from at least k−1 others with respect to its quasi-identifiers.
-	l-diversity and t-closeness — refinements of k-anonymity: l-diversity (Machanavajjhala et al., 2007) requires each equivalence class to contain at least l well-represented values of the sensitive attribute; t-closeness (Li, Li and Venkatasubramanian, 2007) requires the class's sensitive-attribute distribution to stay within a threshold t of the distribution across the full dataset.
-	SSAID (Secure Settings Android ID) — a per-application-scoped device identifier on Android, relevant to device fingerprinting.
-	Advertising ID — a user-resettable identifier provided by the mobile OS for advertising, which platform policy prohibits linking to hardware identifiers.
-	SDK (Software Development Kit) — a third-party code library integrated into an application; a common source of covert data collection.
-	JA3 / JA4 — network-fingerprinting methods that hash the fields of a TLS Client Hello into a compact identifier of the client software.
-	Pseudonymisation — processing that prevents data being attributed to a specific person without additional information (e.g. a key). Under the GDPR, encrypted data is pseudonymised, not anonymised, and remains personal data.
-	Cryptographic erasure — secure deletion achieved by destroying the key that protects data, rendering the ciphertext permanently unreadable without overwriting it.
-	SBOM (Software Bill of Materials) — an inventory of the components and dependencies in a piece of software, used to assess supply-chain exposure.
-	GDPR / HIPAA / OWASP — General Data Protection Regulation (EU 2016/679) / Health Insurance Portability and Accountability Act (US) / Open Worldwide Application Security Project.
 1. Introduction
1.1 Background
The smartphone has become the primary repository for the most sensitive artefacts of a person’s identity. Passports, driving licences, medical reports, insurance documents, banking correspondence and academic certificates are routinely photographed and retained on mobile devices, where they frequently sit in the general camera roll protected by nothing stronger than a device-level lock screen. This concentration of high-value personal data on a single, portable, network-connected and easily lost device represents one of the defining security and privacy challenges of the mobile-first era.
The financial and human consequences of failing to protect such data are substantial and well documented. IBM’s Cost of Data Breach Report 2025 places the global average cost of a breach at $4.44 million, rising to a record $10.22 million in the United States, with customer Personally Identifiable Information (PII) the single most frequently compromised data category, involved in 53% of breaches. Healthcare — the sector most directly relevant to medical documents users store on their phones — remains the most expensive sector at $7.42 million per breach, a position it has held for over a decade (IBM, 2025). These figures establish that the data class this project is concerned with is precisely the data class attackers value most.
It was against the backdrop that, in the preceding academic year[PA1.1], the authors designed and built SecDocVault: a cross-platform mobile application intended to remove sensitive documents from insecure general storage and place them inside an encrypted vault. The application encrypts document contents using AES-256-GCM, derives keys from user credentials using Password-Based Key Derivation Function 2 (PBKDF2), protects its master key using the Android Keystore, and relies on Firebase for authentication and storage, with biometric or PIN unlocking and automatic logout on inactivity. SecDocVault was presented, as such applications invariably are, with a set of implicit and explicit promises to users: that their documents are confidential, that only they can access them, and that the service itself cannot read what it stores. This project takes those promises as its subject of inquiry. Since SecDocVault is the authors' own work, this project is able to subject it to full offensive testing with the clearcut authorisation that ethical security assessment requires — an access position rarely available when evaluating third-party applications.
1.2 Problem definition
The central problem this research addresses is the gap between the privacy and security guarantees that mobile applications claim to provide and the guarantees they actually deliver. Adopting a recognised cryptographic algorithm does not mean it is secure; presenting an application as private does not mean it cannot be linked to its user. The distance between these positions is where real users are harmed, and it is a distance that is rarely measured against a single application in a rigorous, end-to-end manner.
There is considerable evidence that this gap is real and widespread.
1.	Industry analysis by NowSecure has found that more than 85% of tested applications violate at least one of the Open Worldwide Application Security Project (OWASP) Mobile Top 10 risk categories, with roughly half exhibiting insecure data storage (NowSecure, cited in Zaitseva et al., 2023[PA2.1]).
2.	More recently, an Enterprise Strategy Group survey commissioned by Guardsquare (2025) reported a striking contradiction in organisational confidence. While 93% of organisations believed their mobile application protections were sufficient, 62% had nonetheless suffered at least one mobile application security incident in the preceding year, averaging nine incidents each, at an average cost of $6.99 million per mobile breach (Enterprise Strategy Group, 2025).
3.	The 2024 version of the OWASP Mobile Top 10 elevated Improper Credential Usage (M1) to the top position and retained Insecure Data Storage (M2→M9) and Insufficient Cryptography (M5→M10) as core risks, even though they had fallen down the rankings (OWASP, 2024).
4.	The Verizon 2025 Data Breach Investigations Report reinforces the point from the credential angle, attributing 88% of web and application breaches to use stolen credentials (Verizon, 2025).
All this maps directly onto the design decisions embodied in SecDocVault.
Beyond confidentiality of content, the problem has a second dimension: the identifiability of the user. An application can encrypt every document flawlessly and still betray who its users are, where they are, and what they do, through device fingerprinting, metadata leakage in stored files[PA3.1], observable network traffic, and embedded analytics Software Development Kits (SDKs). A related question is whether data, which a user believes they have deleted, is genuinely irrecoverable, or whether it remains as forensic remanence on the device or in the cloud backend.
The impact of these failures is both technical, and financial. Under the General Data Protection Regulation (GDPR), inadequate technical and organisational measures, unlawful processing and improper handling of data subject rights carry penalties of up to €20 million or 4% of global annual turnover, whichever is greater (European Union, 2016). Enforcement is neither theoretical nor declining: cumulative GDPR fines since 2018 exceed €7.1 billion, with approximately €1.2 billion imposed in 2025 alone. European data protection authorities now receive an average of 443 breach notifications per day, a 22% year-on-year increase (DLA Piper, 2026). For an application handling health-related documents, the United States Health Insurance Portability and Accountability Act (HIPAA) imposes a parallel and equally consequential regime. An application that mishandles the data it was built to protect can expose its users to fraud, identity theft and loss of privacy, while its operators suffer severe financial consequences and reputational damage.
This project situates itself deliberately at the intersection of three domains: security, forensics, and ethical circumvention. It is a work of security, which examines the cryptographic and architectural defences of a live application; of forensics, which investigates metadata leakage, data remanence and the recoverability of supposedly deleted information; and of ethical circumvention, which applies offensive penetration-testing techniques to bypass the application’s own controls in controlled, authorised setting in order to expose and remediate weaknesses.
1.3 Aims
The project pursues three connected aims:
-	To develop a rigorous, research-grounded understanding of what privacy and data/document protection requires in a modern mobile application — examined simultaneously through the legal-regulatory lens, the cryptographic lens, and the anonymity and digital-fingerprinting lens.
-	To determine whether SecDocVault honours the confidentiality, access-control and privacy guarantees it presents to its users, by subjecting it to a systematic, reproducible security assessment.
-	To measure the gap between promise and reality, and to derive from that measurement concrete, transferable best-practice guidance for the development of privacy-sensitive mobile applications.
1.4 Objectives
These aims are operationalised through the following objectives:
-	Conduct a structured literature review spanning data protection law and privacy-enhancing technologies, applied cryptography and key management, and the theory and practice of anonymity and device fingerprinting.
-	Construct a formal threat model of SecDocVault using the STRIDE methodology, establishing its attack surface, trust boundaries and adversary assumptions.
-	Perform a multi-technique security assessment including static analysis, dynamic testing, cryptographic validation, authentication and session testing, access-control evaluation, and backend (Firebase) configuration review.
-	Investigate the application’s privacy posture specifically: audit stored documents for EXIF and metadata leakage, enumerate embedded analytics and third-party SDKs, and test whether deleted documents can be forensically recovered from the device or backend.
-	Assess and prioritise all findings using the Common Vulnerability Scoring System (CVSS), and propose specific remediation for each.
-	Evaluate the application’s alignment with the technical requirements of GDPR and HIPAA, and compare the application’s stated guarantees against the assessment results.
1.5 Hypothesis
This project advances the following central hypothesis:
A mobile document vault that correctly adopts strong, standardised cryptographic primitives can still fail to meet its stated privacy and security guarantees, because the material weaknesses in such systems lie mostly within implementation detail, configuration, metadata handling and user identifiability rather than in the choice of cryptographic algorithm itself.
If the hypothesis holds, the security assessment will reveal exploitable or privacy-eroding weaknesses that exist despite — not because of — SecDocVault’s use of AES-256-GCM, PBKDF2 and the Android Keystore. If it is disproved, the application’s guarantees will prove robust across the implementation, configuration, metadata and anonymity dimensions when tested. Either outcome constitutes a meaningful result.
1.6 Research questions
The research is guided by four questions, the first three corresponding to the project’s three strands and the fourth to their synthesis:
RQ1	Anonymity and digital fingerprinting
To what extent can a user of SecDocVault be identified, tracked or anonymised — through device fingerprinting, metadata leakage or traffic analysis — notwithstanding the encryption of document content. Secondly, is data the user has deleted genuinely unrecoverable?
RQ2	Privacy and data protection
Where does encryption stop being sufficient for lawful privacy, and which  data protection obligations does a cryptographically sound document vault still fail to meet? 
RQ3	Cryptography and implementation
What sources of cryptographic primitives are considered secure, what factors influence that statement?
RQ4	Synthesis
Where does the gap between the application’s stated privacy and security promises and its observed behaviour actually lie, and what best practices most effectively close it?
1[PA4.1].7 Contributions
This thesis makes the following contributions:
-	An integrated, three-lens treatment of privacy — legal, cryptographic, and anonymity/fingerprinting — applied to a single concrete application, where these perspectives are ordinarily examined in isolation from one another.
-	A documented and reproducible security assessment of a real cross-platform mobile document vault, comprising a full threat model, a catalogue of findings with CVSS severity scores, and mapped remediation guidance.
-	Empirical evidence on the theory-versus-practice gap in privacy-marked and supposedly zero-knowledge mobile applications, contributing to the broader understanding of why cryptographically sound systems still fail their users.
-	Practical, transferable guidance for developers of privacy-sensitive mobile applications, including anti-fingerprinting measures, metadata hygiene and secure-deletion practices that are frequently overlooked in mainstream secure-development advice.

2A[PA6.1]. Literature Review: Anonymity & Digital Fingerprinting[PA7.1]
SecDocVault is presented to its users as a private space for sensitive documents. Its encryption of document content, examined elsewhere in this review, addresses only one half of that promise. Confidentiality of content says nothing about whether the person using the application can be identified, whether their activity can be linked across time, or whether material they believe they have destroyed genuinely ceases to exist. This chapter surveys the research that bears on those questions. It begins with the conceptual vocabulary that allows anonymity to be stated precisely, turns to the evidence that anonymisation is far more fragile than it appears, and then examines the concrete techniques by which a mobile device and its user can be fingerprinted, tracked and de-anonymised. It closes with the literature on metadata leakage and data remanence, both of which determine whether a vault can honour the privacy guarantees it implies. Throughout, the aim is not to catalogue what each study did but to weigh what it establishes, where its limitations lie, and how it bears on the assessment of SecDocVault that follows.
2A.1 Conceptual foundations: anonymity, pseudonymity, unlinkability, unobservability
Any claim that an application is "private" is only meaningful once the sense of privacy is fixed. The most widely adopted vocabulary for doing so is that of Pfitzmann and Hansen (2010), whose consolidated terminology has become the reference point for privacy-property definitions. They define:
-	Anonymity as the state of a subject being unidentifiable within a set of subjects, the anonymity set, so that anonymity strengthens as the set grows and the subject becomes harder to single out.
-	Unlinkability describes a situation in which an observer cannot determine whether two items of interest, such as two sessions or two requests, relate to the same entity.
-	Undetectability and the stronger unobservability concern whether an observer can tell that an item of interest exists at all.
-	Pseudonymity occupies the middle ground, in which a subject acts under a persistent identifier that is not bound to their real-world identity.
Applying this framework to SecDocVault clarifies what the application can and cannot claim. In realistic terms, it offers confidentiality of document content, that is, its contents are kept secret, together with pseudonymity, since each user is represented by a Firebase account associated with an email address. It does not offer unlinkability, because account identifiers and observable traffic tie a user’s sessions together, and it does not offer unobservability, because a network observer can detect that the application is in use. It matters to state the guarantee at this level of precision, because the empirical work in later chapters is only interesting insofar as it measures the anonymity that the application actually provides.
The principal limitation of Pfitzmann and Hansen for this project is that their framework is definitional rather than operational. It supplies a precise language for stating a privacy property but no method for verifying that a given system possesses it, and that verification gap is exactly what the assessment of SecDocVault sets out to fill. 
The framework also predates the era of pervasive mobile fingerprinting. Reasoning about an anonymity set implicitly assumes a bounded population of distinguishing features, an assumption that the always-on identifiers of a modern smartphone undermine. The sections that follow show why that assumption no longer holds.
2A.2 Quantifying anonymity: k-anonymity and its limits
Where Pfitzmann and Hansen supply qualitative definitions, Sweeney (2002) offers a quantitative model that has shaped two decades of privacy engineering. Her notion of k-anonymity holds that a released dataset protects its subjects if the information for each individual cannot be distinguished from that of at least k-1 others with respect to the quasi-identifiers, i.e. combinations of attributes such as postcode, gender and date of birth that are not unique identifiers in isolation but become identifying in combination. This model underpins the Datafly and µ-Argus systems and is often cited alongside Sweeney’s striking empirical finding that roughly 87% of the United States population could be uniquely identified from the single triple of five-digit ZIP code, gender and date of birth, a result she illustrated by re-identifying the medical record of the Governor of Massachusetts from a public voter roll.
The relevance to SecDocVault lies in any telemetry or analytics the application emits. Attributes such as device model, operating-system version, locale, screen geometry and timezone constitute precisely the kind of quasi-identifier set Sweeney describes, and an uncommon combination of them drives the effective value of k towards 1, at which point the user is unique and the notional protection collapses. The lesson is that hashing or nominally anonymising such data does little if the underlying attribute combination is itself rare.
However, k-anonymity is a necessary rather than a sufficient condition, and Sweeney’s own later critics established as much. The model is vulnerable to homogeneity attacks, where all records in an equivalence class share the same sensitive value, and to background-knowledge attacks, which motivated the later refinement. More fundamentally, the guarantee weakens sharply as the number of attributes grows, and it is this failure on high-dimensional data that the next section develops into a general result.
The refinements that followed are worth stating precisely, because they mark out both the progress and the limits of syntactic anonymity. Machanavajjhala et al. (2007) answered the homogeneity and background-knowledge attacks with l-diversity, which strengthens k-anonymity by requiring that the records in each equivalence class be not merely indistinguishable on their quasi-identifiers but also "well-represented" by at least l distinct values of the sensitive attribute, so that an adversary who narrows a target to a single group still cannot infer that subject's sensitive value with confidence. Li, Li and Venkatasubramanian (2007) showed in turn that l-diversity is neither necessary nor sufficient: it remains open to skewness and similarity attacks that exploit the distribution of the sensitive attribute itself, and they proposed t-closeness, which requires that the distribution of a sensitive attribute within any equivalence class differ from its distribution across the whole table by no more than a threshold t. The trajectory is instructive rather than reassuring, because each refinement closes a specific attack only by imposing a heavier generalisation or suppression cost on the data, and none escapes the underlying tension that the next section makes explicit: on rich, high-dimensional data the very attributes that make a record useful are the attributes that make it identifiable. For SecDocVault the lesson is not that the application should implement t-closeness, which is a data-publishing technique rather than a client control, but that any attempt to "anonymise" telemetry by generalisation inherits this diminishing-returns problem, so a claim of anonymised analytics cannot be taken at face value without knowing which model, at which parameter, has actually been applied.
2A.3 De-anonymisation / re-identification
The standard demonstration that anonymisation and anonymity are not the same is the work of Narayanan and Shmatikov (2008) on the Netflix Prize dataset. Presented with the ratings of 500,000 subscribers, from which direct identifiers had been removed, they showed that an adversary possessing only a small amount of auxiliary knowledge, drawn in their case from the public Internet Movie Database (IMDb), could locate a target’s record with high confidence. Crucially, their technique tolerates noise and perturbation in the data and imperfect knowledge on the part of the adversary, which is what makes it a general statistical attack on high-dimensional, sparse micro-data rather than a quirk of one dataset.
Because that result is now well over a decade old, it is open to the objection that modern practice has moved on. Rocher, Hendrickx and de Montjoye (2019) close that line of retreat. Using a generative copula-based model evaluated across 210 populations, they estimate that 99.98% of Americans would be correctly re-identified in any dataset using only 15 demographic attributes, achieving predictive scores for individual uniqueness between 0.84 and 0.97. They argue directly that heavily sampled, de-identified datasets are unlikely to satisfy the anonymisation standard set by the GDPR, which places the finding squarely within the regulatory context this thesis engages. Taken together, the two studies show that the fragility of anonymisation is neither historical nor incidental but a persistent property of rich data.
The critical qualification for the present work is that both attacks assume the availability of an auxiliary dataset against which the target data can be linked, and both are strongest on sparse, high-dimensional data. A single-application vault does not obviously supply such an auxiliary channel, and an honest threat model for SecDocVault must ask where a linking dataset would come from before importing the full force of these results. That scoping, rather than an uncritical citation of the headline percentages, is the assessment the review is obliged to make; the value of the studies here is the principle that any linkable side-data can re-attach identity even when document content is never exposed.
2A.4 Fingerprinting: foundational (browser) work and why it transfers
Device fingerprinting generalises the insight of the previous section from released datasets to live clients. The foundational study is Eckersley (2010), whose Panopticlick experiment collected fingerprints from 470,161 browsers and found that the distribution carried at least 18.1 bits of entropy, enough that a browser picked at random would, on average, share its fingerprint with only one in 286,777 others. Among browsers exposing Flash or Java, 94.2% were unique. The enduring contribution is the principle rather than the figures: a set of individually innocuous attributes, such as user-agent string, installed fonts, screen resolution and plug-in list, aggregates into a stable and stateless identifier that requires neither cookies nor special permissions. Eckersley also examined how fingerprints change over time and showed that even a simple heuristic could recognise an evolved fingerprint as belonging to a previously seen browser in the large majority of cases.
Laperdrix et al. (2020) bring this picture up to date in a peer-reviewed survey that documents the vectors which emerged after Eckersley, including canvas, WebGL and audio-context fingerprinting, and that systematises the defences proposed against them. Their account is useful here for two reasons: it confirms that the aggregation principle scales well beyond the 2010 setting, and it provides a scholarly counterweight to the industry sources discussed below, allowing the network-fingerprinting claims to be triangulated against peer-reviewed work.
Neither study should be read uncritically. Eckersley’s sample was self-selected and skewed towards privacy-conscious and technically sophisticated visitors, which very likely overstates the uniqueness that would be observed in a general population, and the drift he documented means that a snapshot of uniqueness is not the same as durable trackability over time. Laperdrix et al. note in turn that the available defences remain fragmented and can be self-defeating, since a countermeasure adopted by too few users itself becomes a distinguishing feature. The work is browser-centric; the question for this thesis is what a mobile application, rather than a browser, exposes, and that is the subject of the next section.
2A.5 Mobile fingerprinting vectors
The aggregation principle transfers directly from the browser to the mobile application, and in several respects the mobile setting is worse, because a handset exposes richer and more stable signals than a browser and does so to any application with which the user has chosen to engage. The literature can be read as a layered account of those signals.
2A.5.1 OS/account identifiers (SSAID and advertising ID)
At the level of operating-system and account identifiers, the platform vendors have introduced mitigations that exist chiefly on paper. Since Android 8.0 the Secure Settings Android ID (SSAID) is scoped per application-signing key, which limits naive cross-application linking, and the advertising identifier is user-resettable and, by policy, is not to be joined to hardware identifiers. Whether an application and its embedded SDKs actually respect these boundaries is an empirical question. Wu et al. (2016) showed why these mitigations are insufficient in principle: by combining 38 implicit identifiers that could be read without requesting any permission, they constructed a device fingerprint and evaluated it on 50,830 fingerprints drawn from 2,239 devices, achieving stable identification with no permissions at all. The obvious limitation here is that their study predates several subsequent Android privacy changes, so the specific signals need to be re-tested on a current platform rather than assuming that they remain readable.
2A.5.2 Sensor fingerprinting
At the level of hardware, Dey et al. (2014) demonstrated a still more troubling vector for fingerprinting. Their AccelPrint system exploits the minute manufacturing imperfections of smartphone accelerometers, which cause different devices to respond measurably differently to the same stimulus, to derive a fingerprint that identifies a device with better than 96% precision and recall across a pool of 80 accelerometer chips and 25 Android phones. Because the fingerprint is rooted in physical hardware, it survives reinstallation and factory reset, which makes it close to a worst case for unlinkability. The study’s limitations are those of its period: it was conducted under controlled conditions on a small and now dated device pool, and access to motion sensors has since become more tightly governed on modern Android, so its present-day exploitability is a matter for measurement rather than assumption.
2A.5.3 Configuration fingerprinting
At the level of software configuration, Kurtz et al. (2016) established that hardware identifiers need not be available at all. Querying 29 configuration features reflecting the user’s own personalisation of the device, exposed to third-party applications through the official iOS SDK, they re-identified approximately 97% of around 8,000 devices even after hardware identifiers have been removed. The finding shows that a device’s personalised configuration reconstitutes a stable fingerprint on its own. The study is specific to iOS and to 2016, so its value for the present work lies in prompting an equivalent enumeration of the configuration signals still readable on current Android devices.
2A.5.4 Network / TLS fingerprinting (JA3/JA4)
At the level of the network, fingerprinting persists even when traffic is encrypted. The JA3 method introduced by Althouse et al. (2017) hashes the ordered fields of the TLS Client Hello, its version, cipher suites, extensions and elliptic-curve parameters, into a compact client fingerprint, and its successor JA4+, published by FoxIO (2023), sorts those fields for stability and extends coverage to QUIC. The practical consequence is that a passive observer can distinguish and potentially link a client from the shape of its handshake without decrypting anything. These are industry rather than peer-reviewed sources, and they must be read with the corresponding caution: JA3 in particular is order-dependent and therefore unstable, and because the fingerprint reflects the underlying TLS library it is shared across every application built on that library, which creates a real risk of false linkage. This is precisely why the survey of Laperdrix et al. is used here as corroboration.
2A.5.5 SDKs and covert/side channels (how identifiers actually leak)
Finally, the mechanism by which these identifiers are actually harvested in the wild is documented by Reardon et al. (2019). Their large-scale study, which won a distinguished-paper award, found hundreds of applications and third-party SDKs obtaining unique identifiers and geolocation data through covert and side channels that circumvent the Android permission model altogether. The relevance to SecDocVault is immediate, given its reliance on Firebase and the likelihood of embedded analytics, and the work justifies enumerating the application’s own dependencies rather than reasoning about them in the abstract. Its own limitation, common to such measurement studies, is that it captures a particular generation of the platform, so it motivates fresh testing rather than settling the matter.
2A.6 Metadata leakage — EXIF/GPS in stored documents
A vault may encrypt a document’s bytes flawlessly and still betray its user through the metadata carried inside the document or left in the caches and thumbnails that surround it. Friedland and Sommer (2010) demonstrated the real-world consequences of this in their study of geotagging, which they termed cybercasing. Photographs and videos captured on smartphones routinely embed EXIF metadata recording device model, timestamp and, where location services are enabled, precise GPS coordinates. The authors recovered home addresses and other sensitive locations by extracting this data from images posted to Craigslist, Twitter and YouTube, noting that an iPhone 3G could locate a photograph to within about a metre. Because SecDocVault ingests images of documents, the originating photographs may carry exactly this class of metadata, and if the application’s thumbnails, caches or analytics ever handle those files in unencrypted form, it leaks location and device identity in spite of encrypting the payload.
The study must be read with its age in mind. It predates the now-common practice by large web platforms of stripping EXIF data server-side on upload, so the residual risk today is concentrated at the client level and is application-specific rather than platform-wide. That, however, strengthens rather than weakens the case for testing SecDocVault directly, since whether metadata survives ingestion and storage is a property of the application’s own handling and cannot be inferred from platform behaviour.
2A.7 Data remanence & secure deletion (bridges to GDPR right to erasure)
The final strand of the review concerns whether data a user has deleted is genuinely irrecoverable, a question that connects the technical assessment to the right to erasure under Article 17 of the GDPR. The organising reference is the systematisation of Reardon, Basin and Capkun (2013), which surveys secure deletion across the storage stack and offers a taxonomy of adversaries distinguished by their capabilities together with a classification of deletion approaches. Any specific claim about deletion in SecDocVault must be located within this framework, in particular the question of at which layer erasure is supposed to occur.
The physical reason that naive deletion fails is documented by Wei et al. (2011), who show that flash-based solid-state drives do not overwrite in place. The flash translation layer writes new data to fresh cells and defers reclamation, so a logical delete or a single-file sanitisation leaves the original content recoverable until the underlying block is eventually reused. Building on the same property of flash, Reardon, Capkun and Basin (2012) proposed the Data Node Encrypted File System (DNEFS), which achieves efficient secure deletion by cryptographic erasure, destroying the key rather than attempting to overwrite the data, and demonstrate it within the Android operating system on a Nexus One handset. Together these works established that secure deletion on a modern device is something that must be deliberately designed rather than assumed to follow from an ordinary delete operation.
The empirical consequences for Android were shown starkly by Simon and Anderson (2015). Studying the factory-reset function on twenty-one handsets from five vendors running Android versions 2.3 to 4.3, they estimated that up to 500 million devices might fail to sanitise the data partition on which credentials and other sensitive data are stored, and up to 630 million might fail to sanitise the internal SD card. They recovered Google credentials on every device that exhibited a flawed reset, and found that even a full-disk-encryption key could be reconstructed from the residue a flawed reset left behind. Shu et al. (2017) corroborated the broader phenomenon, documenting deletion flaws and data remanence in Android file systems more generally.
These studies share a limitation that is itself analytically useful: they largely predate the move to file-based encryption and the use of the TRIM command on modern Android, and cryptographic erasure changes the recovery picture materially. However, the correct response is not to discard these results but to re-test them on a current device, which is the space this project occupies. The findings also carry a direct legal implication that hands off to the data-protection strand of the thesis. If documents a user has deleted remain recoverable from device storage or from the Firebase backend, then the application cannot honour the right to erasure under Article 17 of the GDPR, and a data-remanence weakness becomes simultaneously a security failure and a compliance failure.
2A.8 Chapter summary & research gap[PA8.1]
The literature reviewed here establishes a coherent progression. Anonymity can be defined precisely (Pfitzmann and Hansen, 2010), yet the anonymisation of rich data is fragile and, on the evidence of Sweeney (2002), Narayanan and Shmatikov (2008) and Rocher et al. (2019), grows more fragile as data become richer. Devices and their users are highly fingerprintable across the browser, sensor, configuration, network and SDK layers, as shown by Eckersley (2010), Dey et al. (2014), Kurtz et al. (2016), Althouse et al. (2017), Laperdrix et al. (2020) and Reardon et al. (2019). Documents leak location and device identity through metadata (Friedland and Sommer, 2010), and deletion frequently fails on flash storage and on Android in particular (Wei et al., 2011; Reardon et al., 2012, 2013; Simon and Anderson, 2015; Shu et al., 2017).
What this body of work does not provide is an integrated assessment. Its findings are distributed across web browsers, generic Android builds and public datasets, and a substantial part of the empirical evidence predates the privacy mitigations of current mobile platforms. There is, in consequence, no study that measures anonymity, fingerprintability and data remanence together for a single, real, encrypted mobile document vault. That is the gap this thesis addresses. SecDocVault serves as the testbed on which these separate threads are unified and the previous, dated results are re-examined on a current device, allowing the distance between the application’s stated privacy guarantees and its observed behaviour to be measured and fed back into concrete remediation. The methodology chapter sets out how each of the works reviewed here maps onto a specific, reproducible test of the application.
2B. Literature Review: Privacy & Data Protection
2B.1 Framing: why data-protection law governs a document vault
The artefacts SecDocVault is built to hold — passports, driving licences, medical reports, bank statements, insurance correspondence and academic certificates — are personal data in the strict legal sense, and a substantial proportion of them are special-category data. Whatever privacy the application delivers is therefore not only an engineering property but a legal obligation, and data-protection law is the second of the three lenses through which this thesis examines the gap between the guarantees the application makes and those it keeps. Two regimes are directly engaged. Within the European Union, Regulation (EU) 2016/679, the General Data Protection Regulation (GDPR), governs any processing of the personal data of individuals in the Union (European Union, 2016), and its material scope is triggered the moment an identifiable person’s document is ingested, encrypted, stored or shared. Where the stored material is health-related — and medical reports are a use case the application itself advertises — the data becomes a special category under Article 9, attracting a heightened regime; in the United States the parallel obligations of the Health Insurance Portability and Accountability Act (HIPAA) attach to protected health information. This section reviews the principles, obligations and scholarly critique that define what “data protection” requires of an application of this kind, and in doing so establishes the criteria against which the empirical assessment in later chapters measures SecDocVault’s compliance posture.
One conceptual point must be settled at the outset, because it conditions everything that follows. It is tempting to treat an encrypted, “zero-knowledge” vault as though encryption places the data beyond the reach of data-protection law. It does not. As Section 2B.4 develops, encrypted personal data remains personal data, and the full weight of the regulatory obligations continues to bear on the operator of the service. The legal lens therefore does not ask merely “is the data encrypted?” but a wider set of questions — is collection minimised, is deletion real, is processing accountable — that a purely cryptographic evaluation would miss entirely.
2B.2 The data-protection principles and their design consequences
Article 5 of the GDPR sets out the principles that govern all processing, and each maps onto a concrete design constraint for a document vault. Lawfulness, fairness and transparency require an identifiable legal basis and honest communication to the user about what becomes of their data. Purpose limitation confines processing to the purpose for which data was collected — for a vault, storage and retrieval — and warns against secondary uses such as analytics or profiling. Data minimisation, the requirement that data be adequate, relevant and limited to what is necessary, is the principle most frequently violated in mobile software, because embedded analytics and third-party SDKs habitually gather device and usage data far in excess of what a storage function needs; this is the same collection surface the anonymity-and-fingerprinting strand of this thesis (Chapter 2A) interrogates. Storage limitation forbids retention beyond necessity and therefore presupposes a working deletion mechanism, connecting directly to the right to erasure discussed in Section 2B.5. Integrity and confidentiality — the security principle — is the explicit hook for encryption and access control. Accountability, finally, obliges the controller to demonstrate compliance rather than merely assert it, and it is precisely this demonstrate-don’t-assert standard that a reproducible security assessment is well placed to test.
The principles are deliberately technology-neutral, which is at once their strength and, for an engineer, their limitation: they state what must be achieved but not how, and the distance between a stated principle and its implementation is exactly where compliance failures accumulate. For SecDocVault the security principle appears well served on paper by AES-256-GCM and the Android Keystore, but minimisation, purpose limitation and storage limitation are governed by application behaviour — what the client collects, what it transmits to Firebase, and whether deleted material truly disappears — none of which follows from the choice of cipher. This gap between principle and implementation is what the more operational literature reviewed next attempts to close.
2B.3 Data protection by design and by default (Article 25) and privacy engineering
The most direct legal expression of “build privacy in from the start” is Article 25 of the GDPR, which imposes data protection by design and by default. Article 25(1) requires the controller to implement appropriate technical and organisational measures — data minimisation is the article’s own example — designed to give effect to the data-protection principles; Article 25(2) requires that, by default, only the personal data necessary for each specific purpose be processed, and that personal data not be made accessible to an indefinite number of people without the individual’s intervention. The intellectual ancestor of this provision is the Privacy by Design framework articulated by Cavoukian (2011), whose seven foundational principles hold, among other things, that privacy should be proactive rather than reactive, embedded in design rather than bolted on, and the default setting rather than an option the user must discover and enable. The framework’s influence on Article 25 is widely acknowledged and it has become the reference vocabulary for privacy engineering.
The principal difficulty with Privacy by Design, for a project that must actually test an artefact, is that it is aspirational rather than operational. Its principles state goals — “privacy as the default,” “full lifecycle protection” — without prescribing verifiable criteria, and recent critical work argues that such “by design” slogans are too abstract to be enforced and too easily satisfied on paper (Ruohonen, 2025). The gap between the principle and a checkable property is the same gap the European Union Agency for Cybersecurity sought to bridge in its influential report on translating privacy from policy into engineering, which maps the legal requirements onto concrete privacy-enhancing technologies and design strategies (Danezis et al., 2014). That report is valuable precisely because it refuses to treat privacy-enhancing technologies as a panacea to be layered onto a finished system, insisting instead that they be selected against a threat model — the discipline this thesis adopts. Applied to SecDocVault, Article 25 reframes the evaluation question: it is not enough that the application can be configured privately; the default state must itself be protective, which makes the behaviour of guest mode, the default storage location, and any telemetry active on first run legitimate objects of assessment rather than incidental settings.
2B.4 Security of processing (Article 32) and the limits of encryption as compliance
Article 32 requires the controller and processor to implement technical and organisational measures appropriate to the risk, and it names — as examples, not as a fixed checklist — the pseudonymisation and encryption of personal data, the ongoing confidentiality, integrity, availability and resilience of processing systems, the ability to restore data after an incident, and a process for regularly testing and evaluating those measures (European Union, 2016). Two features of this text matter for SecDocVault. First, the standard is risk-proportionate and references the “state of the art,” so a defensible security posture is measured against contemporary practice rather than against a static rule, which is why the currency of the application’s cryptographic dependencies (a matter Chapter 2C examines) is itself a compliance-relevant property. Second, and more consequentially, the article treats regular testing and evaluation as an obligation in its own right — meaning that the kind of assessment this project performs is not merely good practice but is itself the activity Article 32(1)(d) contemplates.
The deeper contribution of the legal literature here is a corrective to a misconception that a “zero-knowledge” design invites. Encryption, in the vocabulary of the Article 29 Data Protection Working Party’s Opinion 05/2014 on anonymisation techniques, is a form of pseudonymisation, not anonymisation: it is a security measure that reduces the linkability of data to an identity, but so long as a key exists that can restore the plaintext — whether held by the user, the device, or recoverable through the backup passphrase — the data remains identifiable and therefore remains personal data fully within scope of the GDPR (Article 29 Working Party, 2014). The consequence for SecDocVault is precise and important: encrypting every document does not discharge the operator’s obligations, exempt the service from the right of access or erasure, or place it beyond the breach-notification regime. It changes the risk of a breach — encrypted ciphertext leaked without its key is of little use to an attacker — but it does not change the legal character of the data. The Opinion’s own limitation is that it predates the GDPR’s statutory definition of pseudonymisation and speaks in terms of the earlier Directive, so its taxonomy must be read alongside the Regulation; but its central holding, that keyed encryption leaves data identifiable, transfers intact and directly undercuts any claim that a zero-knowledge architecture is, by itself, a complete answer to data-protection compliance.
2B.5 The right to erasure (Article 17) and the deletion problem
Article 17 grants the data subject the right to obtain erasure of their personal data without undue delay in defined circumstances, the so-called right to be forgotten. For a document vault the right is not exotic: a user who deletes a stored passport reasonably expects the copy to be gone, from the device and from the backend alike. The legal test, however, is satisfied only by genuine irrecoverability, and this is where the data-protection strand rejoins the forensic findings of Section 2A.7. If a document a user has deleted can still be recovered from device storage through flash remanence, or persists in a Firebase backup or replica, then the application fails Article 17 in substance however convincingly the user interface reports success — and the same fact is simultaneously a security weakness and a compliance failure.
The scholarly literature is candid about how hard real erasure is in modern architectures. Politou et al. (2018) examine the “uneasy relationship” between the right to be forgotten and the backup and archiving procedures that mainstream security standards actively require, showing that the redundancy engineered into resilient systems — snapshots, replicas, archival copies — works against a guarantee of deletion, and that erasure must be designed to reach every storage layer if it is to be lawful rather than merely apparent. Their analysis is largely conceptual and pre-dates widespread adoption of cryptographic erasure, in which destroying a key renders the data it protected permanently unreadable; that technique, already encountered in Section 2A.7, is the most promising reconciliation of resilient backups with a right to erasure, and its presence or absence in SecDocVault is therefore a specific, testable question rather than an assumption. The convergence is what makes this the strongest bridge between the project’s legal and technical strands: a single experiment — attempting forensic recovery of a deleted document from the device and the backend — yields evidence that speaks at once to a security property and to an Article 17 obligation.
2B.6 Special-category and health data: the parallel HIPAA regime
Because the application explicitly contemplates the storage of medical documents, two further layers of obligation attach. Under the GDPR, health data is a special category under Article 9 whose processing is prohibited unless a specific condition is met, and it is treated throughout the Regulation as warranting heightened protection; the right to erasure is correspondingly qualified where national law imposes statutory retention on medical records, a nuance that matters for any future clinical deployment of a vault of this kind. In the United States, the HIPAA Security Rule governs electronic protected health information through administrative, physical and technical safeguards, the last of which include access control, audit controls, integrity protection, authentication and transmission security.
The HIPAA position is currently in flux in a way that strengthens this thesis’s argument rather than weakening it. Under the rule as it has stood since 2013, encryption is an addressable specification — an organisation may decline to encrypt if it documents an equivalent alternative — a flexibility long criticised as, in practice, no flexibility at all, since regulators have consistently treated the absence of encryption as a deficiency. The Notice of Proposed Rulemaking published by the Department of Health and Human Services in January 2025 proposes to remove the addressable/required distinction and elevate encryption of ePHI at rest and in transit, together with multi-factor authentication and regular penetration testing, to mandatory status (U.S. Department of Health and Human Services, 2025). This proposal had not been finalised at the time of writing and its direction may still change, so it must be cited as a proposal rather than settled law; but its trajectory is unambiguous, and it converges with the GDPR’s Article 32 in treating encryption, strong authentication and periodic offensive testing as the expected baseline for sensitive-data systems. The regulatory floor SecDocVault must clear is, on both sides of the Atlantic, rising towards exactly the controls the application claims to implement — which makes the question of whether it implements them correctly the decisive one.
2B.7 Chapter summary & research gap
Read together, the data-protection literature establishes that lawful privacy is a broader property than cryptographic confidentiality. The Article 5 principles demand minimisation, purpose limitation and storage limitation as well as security; Article 25 demands that protection be the default rather than an option; Article 32 demands not only encryption but the ongoing, tested effectiveness of the whole measure set; Article 17 demands that deletion be real to the last storage layer; and the special-category and HIPAA regimes raise the floor further for the health documents the application invites users to store. The recurring theme across these sources is a corrective to the assumption that motivates this thesis’s hypothesis: adopting AES-256-GCM, PBKDF2 and the Android Keystore addresses the security principle but leaves minimisation, default-privacy, erasure and accountability to implementation and configuration, precisely the layer where the earlier evidence shows failures cluster. The Working Party’s reclassification of encryption as pseudonymisation makes the point sharpest of all: a “zero-knowledge” vault does not exit the regulatory regime, it remains fully inside it.
The gap this strand identifies is methodological. The legal scholarship states the obligations in the abstract, and the compliance literature typically evaluates organisations against them on paper — through policies, records and self-assessment — rather than by testing whether a live application’s observable behaviour actually satisfies them. Studies of privacy engineering supply strategies and technologies but are rarely closed against a concrete, adversarially tested artefact; studies of erasure and remanence supply the technical facts but are seldom mapped back onto the specific articles they implicate. What is missing, and what the assessment of SecDocVault supplies, is an end-to-end treatment in which the data-protection requirements are operationalised into concrete tests — a minimisation and SDK audit against Article 5, a default-privacy check against Article 25, a forensic recovery attempt against Article 17, and a controls review against Article 32 and the HIPAA technical safeguards — and evaluated empirically against a single application whose guarantees can be checked rather than assumed. That operationalisation is the contribution of this project’s second strand, and it is the question RQ2 is framed to answer.
2C. Literature Review: Cryptography Deep Dive
2C.1 What is cryptography and its use cases
According to Britannica (2026), cryptography has been around for thousands of years, for most of that period it has not been widely spread as compared to nowadays. It was the tool of the governments, military and diplomatic affairs, but had no standardised algorithms or approaches. Rapid interest in the field was historically spiking during the wars or other political tensions, one of the biggest examples of such is German Enigma machine during WW2 that was used to transfer messages and was eventually broken by a team of English cryptographers led by Alan Turing and Gordon Welchman at Bletchley Park. During the 1960s and 1970s, innovations in digital communication and computing resulted in a vast need of new standardised encryption technologies. This led to the U.S. government's adoption of the Data Encryption Standard (DES) in 1977. Another important landmark in this era became the publication by Diffie and Hellman that introduced the concept of public-key cryptography in 1976. This began adoption of cryptography by computer systems and their dependency on those algorithms.
Menezes et al. define cryptography as a set of  techniques and algorithms of mathematical data manipulation to achieve a variety of goals. These include keeping data secret, determining if it has been altered, verifying identity and determining authorship. Confidentiality, integrity, authentication and non-repudiation principles accordingly. Authors admit that cryptography is not synonymous to information security, it is a set of techniques among several used to achieve the above security objectives. Examples of other techniques involve overall system design, legal action and physical access control.
2C.2 Types of cryptography
Menezes, van Oorschot and Vanstone's handbook (1996). classified cryptography into three groups based on key application nature. Those are unkeyed (hash functions), symmetric-key and public-key primitives. All three are actively applied in the computing industry.
Symmetric-Key Cryptography
In this type of cryptography, the same key is used for both encryption and decryption of the payload, for this reason it must be kept secret at all times. Symmetric-key algorithms divide into two types:
-	Block ciphers divide text into fixed-size blocks and perform operations per-block. If the length of the text or other content doesn’t allow equal division, missing length is achieved with padding which also has different types. For example, if the algorithm is designed to encrypt 36-byte blocks, but the content is 100 bytes, missing 8 bytes needed for clean division by 3 will be padded by adding some content determined by the algorithm used.
-	Stream ciphers are the opposite of block ones, they perform encryption one symbol at a time making them useful when encryption must be bufferless. An example of such is the Vernam cipher, which is the only encryption scheme theoretically proven to be unbreakable with its one time pad if the key is truly random and never reused.
Handbook notes that symmetric-key algorithms offer high performance, reasonable key length and lots of implementation history, but suffer from the difficulty of key distribution and management especially when number of communicating parties increases.
Asymmetric (Public-Key) Cryptography
Public-key cryptography introduced conceptually by Diffie and Hellman (1976) uses a mathematical key pair of public and private keys. The public key can be shared freely with any communication party, it can easily be used for encryption but not for decryption as it is mathematically infeasible. A private key on the other hand, must remain secret at all times as it is used for decryption. These algorithms rely on trapdoor functions that are easy to compute one way, but infeasible the other. Examples are the difficulty of factoring large integers utilised in RSA or the discrete logarithm problem in Diffie-Hellman.
The key advantage of such algorithms is elimination of need for pre-shared keys. This, on the other hand, raises an important issue with the approach, which is the possibility of spoofing, as there is no way to verify who used the public key to encrypt the payload. This drawback allows for man-in-the-middle attacks. For that reason, mechanisms to bind public keys to identities are required in modern infrastructure. This is typically done with the involvement of trusted third parties.
Also, public-key cryptography is generally much slower due to computational difficulty. Additionally, use cases of such are often affected by communication latency during multiple transactions that are required for information exchange. Another drawback is the need for much longer keys compared to symmetric ciphers. However, these approaches are not competing, they rather coexist to guarantee secure communication. Public-key cryptography is used to establish a shared secret which is later used by symmetric algorithms for fast and secure communication.
Hash Functions
As Menezes, van Oorschot and Vanstone (1996) note, the purpose of cryptographic hash function is mapping of input of different length into a fixed-length output (hash value). This is done in a way that ensures that it is infeasible to receive the same output with a different input (collision prevention) and that it is impossible to recover input from output. Hash functions are actively used to make digital signatures more efficient, as only a hash value of a message has to be signed instead of its full content, and data integrity verification happens by recomputing the hash value of a message received. Unkeyed hash functions are purely used for integrity checking and are called modification detection codes (MDCs). Keyed functions on the other hand, also provide origin authentication and are called message authentication codes (MACs).
Digital Signatures
Digital signature is a cryptographic primitive that connects identity to a piece of information, it is an electronic analogue of physical signature. It consists of private and public transformations, one used for signing and known to the signer only and another known to the public for verification. For practical use, signatures must be easy to compute and verify and forgery resistant while valid. A trusted third party is a popular way of managing signatures.
2C.3 Sources of cryptography
Dependencies, plugins, pipelines and applications used in development are the layer of trust most non-technical users rarely think about, even though integrity and safety of those is absolutely crucial in digital security. No matter what application is developed and how it is designed, it will include resources from third parties listed above as it is economically insufficient to redevelop some utility if it already exists and in many cases is open source. This section will mainly focus on supply chain attacks, the malicious approach that targets not the application itself, but the upstream component that it uses. Such incidents are getting more and more common and sophisticated due to the approach of relying on third-party software in modern applications. That kind of attack allows malicious actors to cover multiple services at once by only compromising one dependency that is used in each of them.
In "Reflections on Trusting Trust," Ken Thompson (1984) demonstrated that no matter the amount of source-code analysis conducted, the full safety of a compiled binary is not guaranteed. His proof of concept involved a self-replicating compiler modification which itself was a backdoor injected into the C compiler that would silently insert malicious code into the Unix login program and into any future build of the compiler itself without leaving any trace in target’s source code. This research defined what may be called "transitivity of trust” - security of any entity is defined not only by its own correctness, but also by the correctness of underlying dependencies and software used during its development. For the cryptographic software where minimal changes in the algorithm may deem its encryption or other cryptographic operation worthless, this creates a large and irresolvable attack surface.
Despite its age, Thompson's model still maps directly onto the modern cryptographic supply chain. Take a widely used OpenSSL library for example, its security depends on far more than just source code, aspects like integrity of compiler, linker, build system, CI/CD pipeline and distribution infrastructure may be exploited for subverting cryptographic operations without any noticeable artefact in the source.
Case Study: The Codecov Breach (2021)
In April 2021, Codecov, a widely used code coverage analysis and reporting tool used by thousands of CI/CD pipelines, disclosed that their Bash uploader script had been modified by an unknown threat actor (Codecov, 2021). Modification existed from January 31 until April 1 2021 and caused the script to steal environment variables from the environment where it was executed. Such variables are a common way to insert API keys, credentials, signing keys and other crucial values into applications at runtime.
The attack compromised the docker image creation process and its flow was used to extract credentials of Codecov’s Google Cloud Storage bucket from which the bash uploader was served. By replacing legitimate packages with a trojaned version at the source of distribution, the attacker achieved trusted delivery to all users. From this example, we can see direct correspondence to Thompson's trust transitivity at CI/CD scale both from the user-side and Codecov’s side.
From a cryptographic standpoint, this incident is particularly interesting as some of the leaked environment variables were AWS and other cloud’s access credentials used for cryptographic operations, and tokens granting write access to package signing infrastructure (CISA, 2021). A CISA advisory issued in response stated that one more vector was exploited leading to a subnet of victims using compromised credentials to authenticate software packages. This way, end users had no way of distinguishing between legitimate packages and trojaned ones. This, once again, underlines the relevance of Thompson’s work, as software verification keys became meaningless not due to algorithm incorrectness, but due to compromising the tooling this algorithm was embedded within.
The breach remained undetected for approximately 61 days and the trojaned script was distributed to an estimated 29 000 customers. This showcases a structural problem as conventional analysis used by most teams operates on code repositories and does not cover what happens after the application leaves the developer's side.
Policy and Governance On the Subject: NIST SP 800-161r1 (2022)
NIST Special Publication 800-161 Revision 1, Cybersecurity Supply Chain Risk Management Practices for Systems and Organizations, represents the most authoritative federal guidance on managing the class of risk Thompson identified and Codecov exemplified. Published in May 2022 as response to a wave of security incidents of that nature, the publication describes supply chain risk management (C-SCRM) as a first-class concern within the NIST Cybersecurity and Risk Management Frameworks. It also defined C-SCRM as a process of detecting, assessing and mitigating the risks associated with product and service supply chains. Importantly, it included cryptographic components such as key management services, signing infrastructure and cryptographic libraries within the scope of components requiring specialised control.
The publication mandates supplier risk assessment that includes vendor’s assessment of their own development, build and delivery security practices, precisely the kind of vulnerabilities exploited in Codecov case. One of the key requirements is maintenance of a Software Bill of Materials (SBOM) by organisations of their critical components, enabling rapid impact assessment and threat mitigation. This measure was reasoned by some organisations struggling to identify exposure even after vendor’s disclosure.
Publication also focuses on provenance - ensuring that software was not modified in transit from publisher to client. Once again, it is directly exemplified by Codecov’s case. SP 800-161r1 recommends code signing with hardware security modules, hash verification, and reproducible builds as technical controls to enforce this property.
Despite its practicality, SP 800-161r1 operates at organisational and process levels struggling to mitigate the embedded structural problem identified by Thompson. It identifies such practices as supplier verification, build processes audit, anomalies detection and duplicate builds with different tools.  However, it struggles to suggest a way to identify trusted verification without a hardware-backed process. This remains an active area of research.
2C.4 Computational Cost of Cryptographic Choice: Performance-Oriented Review
A traditional assumption of cryptography is that speed of cryptographic operations directly correlates to security, such that the faster is the algorithm, the less security it provides. However, research produced by Bernstein,  Lange, and their collaborators over the past two decades embodies the most systematic counter argument to that statement. The ECRYPT Benchmarking of Cryptographic Systems (eBACS) project proposed  that its algorithm design choices primarily determined the cryptographic throughput, not hardware. 
This section will conduct a performance-oriented review on cryptography use cases based on the methodology used by eBACS benchmark, primitive-level performance and offer algorithm selection in real-world application context.
The eBACS project provides continuously updated measurements for cryptographic primitives across a range of hardware differing in architecture. Its scope covers all kinds of cryptography primitives including elliptic-curve signature and post-quantum candidates submitted to the NIST Post-Quantum Cryptography standardisation process. The main methodology point in this project is reproducibility, as all tests are conducted using a single software suite (SUPERCOP toolkit) and results are published raw, not summarised. This differs from vendor-supplied figures as they often selectively show only best-case scenarios. For that reason, Bernstein and Lange argue that analysis of NIST's elliptic-curve standards without independent verification of performance data has no basis for fair algorithm comparison (Bernstein and Lange, 2013).
Key Findings on Primitives Performance
Public-Key Primitives
The introduction of Ed25519 in Bernstein et al. (2012) showcased the importance of eBACS programme's central thesis, as this algorithm built on top of Curve25519 using SHA-512 as its internal hash performed an average of one to two times faster than its opponent RSA-2048 signing on identical Intel hardware. Performance advantage did not arise from reduced security properties, as the above algorithm targeted 128-bit security, equivalent to RSA-3072 which took approximately 100 times longer signing on the same hardware.
Symmetric Primitives and Authenticated Encryption
Similarly, the authenticated encryption scheme standardised in RFC 8439, ChaCha20-Poly1305, significantly outperforms AES-GCM on hardware without AES-specific acceleration. Most modern devices lack such acceleration. On ARM Cortex-A8 measurements, ChaCha20 achieves about 3.7 cycles per byte against 7.8 cycles per byte for AES-128-CTR. This also showcases that algorithm choice is not hardware agnostic and may lead to significant  performance differences (2-3 times)which is hugely important in embedded and high throughput components.
Hash Functions
Hash functions performance rates in eBACS and work by Aumasson et al. (2013) describe the appearance of BLAKE family hashing algorithms as their popularity dominates when it comes to software hashing solutions. The most popular variant of such is BLAKE2b, which arrives at about 3.08 cycles per byte against 8.9 cycles per byte by SHA-256 on identical hardware. In speed, it is roughly comparable to MD5 which has been deprecated for a long time for security reasons. In 2020, BLAKE3 was introduced utilising parallelism for utilising multicore deployments, which allows it to replace legacy hashing algorithms (SHA1, MD5) with limited performance tradeoffs.
Summary on Reviewed Primitives Performance
Across reviewed categories, eBACS data consistently showcases a common pattern: modern algorithms intended to replace legacy options are consistently outperforming those both in speed and security. Thus, the performance cost of upgrading in the majority of cases is either zero or negative.
Algorithm Selection as an Engineering Decision
Bernstein and Lange's critique of the NIST P-curves (Bernstein and Lange, 2013) raises an opinion that lack of publicly available and reproducible benchmarking in NIST’s legacy elliptic-curve algorithms allows for options with suboptimal implementation characteristics like inefficiencies on specific hardware. This demonstrates the cost of the research gap.
A direct reference to supply chain secureness arises from the fact that slow cryptographic operations create pressure on IT engineering teams to weaken or omit some of the cryptographic steps. The eBACS suggests that deployments that adopted modern primitives avoid this pressure as Ed25519 verification at 273,000 cycles has throughput of roughly 8,800 verifications per second on a single core at 2.4 GHz machine, sufficient to verify every artefact in a large build pipeline without considerable latency. The adoption of efficient modern primitives removes a class of justifications for weakened cryptographic hygiene.
However, it must be said that the benchmarking approach to algorithm selection does not take into account multiple important factors that influence deployment. In many cases, other factors like implementation difficulty or network performance affect that decision in a way favouring selection of slower or weaker algorithms. Bernstein et al. (2012) acknowledge this in the Ed25519 paper through the inclusion of a complete, audited reference implementation alongside the benchmark figures.
2C.5 Post-Quantum Cryptography
Shor (1997) demonstrated that quantum machines can factor an integer in O(n³) operations, compared to sub-exponential performance of classical machines. That finding threatens wide-spread public-key primitives such as RSA and discrete-logarithm-based public-key schemes. This finding triggered the harvest-now, decrypt-later threat model, as some records may remain valuable for years. The threat actor acquires ciphertext now and stores it until technology is able  to break its encryption. This triggered years of research into post-quantum cryptography.
Resulting from multi-year research, in 2024, NIST rolled out post-quantum computing standards first proposed by  Ducas et al. (2018). They are now the migration targets, as they offer security proofs and reasonable implementation difficulty.

ML-KEM (Kyber)	Key encapsulation
ML-DSA (Dilithium)	Digital signature
SLH-DSA (SPHINCS+)	Digital signature

For example, Ducas et al. (2018) described: ML-DSA is based on Lyubashevsky's Fiat–Shamir-with-aborts paradigm and achieved SUF-CMA security under Module-LWE and Module-SIS assumptions, both of which are secure from any known quantum attack. Importantly,  keys are under 2 KB, making them compatible with existing TLS certificate chains.
Even with standardisation complete, obstacles remain as ability to swap algorithms without major architectural changes is limited in most systems. Some of the algorithms require hardware-baked features that are not common yet. Lastly, quantum capabilities are not fully researched yet, so the threat persists.
2C.6 Chapter Summary & Research Gap
This chapter described cryptography from different perspectives, commencing in its ancient history and finishing in the post-quantum future. Section 2C.1 covered fundamental objectives targeted by cryptography which are confidentiality, integrity, authentication and non-repudiation.
Section 2C.2 depicts core primitives families defined by Menezes, van Oorschot and Vanstone (1996): symmetric-key ciphers (block and stream variants), asymmetric public-key schemes, hash functions and digital signatures. Each having its trust and performance characteristics resulting in complementary nature of those primitives rather than competing.
Section 2C.3 focused on supply-chain trust as it affects both primitives implementations themselves and cases they are used in. This was shown by Thompson's (1984) proof that source-code correctness does not guarantee binary correctness and showcased by the Codecov breach (2021) as an example of large-scale attack utilising gaps depicted in the section. NIST SP 800-161r1 (2022) was assessed as a governance response, as it offers solutions to reduce risks and damage from that kind of attacks. However, this publication does not provide a full solution to Thompson's structural problem.
Section 2C.4 challenged a paradigm that security and speed characteristics of primitives are proportional, offering eBACS benchmarking programmes led by Bernstein and Lange as proof. It covers all three families of cryptographic primitives by comparing classic algorithms to modern replacements. The data consistently shows how modern replacements outperform legacy counterparts. The section summarizes a conclusion: efficient modern primitives remove engineering pressure to weaken or omit cryptographic steps, thereby strengthening software hygiene as a side-effect of algorithm selection.
Section 2C.5 looked over the future of cryptography and mentioned the future threat that must be acted upon now of the harvest-now, decrypt-later approach. Shor's (1997) O(n³) factorisation algorithm deems modern public-key standards insecure against quantum cryptography. It mentioned NIST’s 2024 standards as the culmination of multi-year research in this area and shift targets. The section also outlines two core complications and threats: complexity of cryptographic transitioning in modern systems and immaturity of quantum systems.
Overall, five subsections reveal a set of problems that were not fully resolved in modern literature. For instance, supply chain and post-quantum areas were not analysed in a bundle, as migration from common primitives to post-quantum introduced the threat of supply-chain related attacks. Secondly, Thompson's transitivity-of-trust problem has no solution that is not hardware-dependent. Lastly, PQC solutions and eBACS benchmarking literature were proceeding rather in parallel than in cooperation. This literature review section is particularly valuable, as it introduces threats and aspects that should be widely considered in modern application development.
